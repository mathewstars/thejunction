# Revontulet: what each measure is, and the study idea behind it

Revontulet is Lanvi's 90-second game: pick a fault line in the ice (easy or hard), trace a half-circle, pull the spark back like a slingshot and let go, tap the embers on a hard shot, then stroke the sleeping fox. The app sends it as a `focus` record with one extra object, `revontulet` (shape and limits: `docs/api/README.md` and `openapi.json`, `RevontuletIn`).

The server stores these numbers and shows them to the clinician. **It does not use the motor and choice measures** to judge a day, pick suggestions or count XP. A record with this object is read as a Revontulet game (see "How a Revontulet game is read" at the end): its reaction times and error rate are counted by Revontulet's own rules and compared only with earlier Revontulet games. Since 14 November it does compare four of them with the person's own earlier Revontulet games, for the clinician page only: see "Own normal" at the end. Nothing here is validated in Lanvi; the studies below are where each idea comes from, with the numbers as the research summary for this game gives them. On 14 November the two effort papers (Treadway 2009 and 2012) and the swipe paper (Chitale 2025) were read on PMC and the rows below corrected; of the tablet handwriting paper (Džepina et al. 2025, Applied Sciences) only the abstract was read, through Crossref: 20 youths with depression and 20 controls wrote the cursive letter "l" repeatedly; 177 features were cut to 3; 82.5% of subjects were sorted correctly. The abstract does not mention semicircles.

| Measure | What it is | Study idea behind it |
|---|---|---|
| `rounds[].effort` | Easy (thin low crack, one shot) or hard (thick vein, two embers, a bigger reward). | The Effort-Expenditure for Rewards Task (EEfRT, Treadway et al. 2009, PLoS ONE): on each of about fifty trials a person picks a low-effort "easy task" for a small fixed reward or a high-effort "hard task" for a higher, variable reward. Among 61 students, those reporting less pleasure chose the hard task a little less often (correlations around −0.3), on some questionnaires and win chances and not on others; one pleasure questionnaire (SHAPS) showed nothing, and hard choices also fell with trial number. Treadway et al. 2012: 20 patients with depression chose the hard task less often than 15 controls; 17 of the patients were on antidepressants, and among the patients a higher depression score went with more hard choices, a longer episode with fewer. The r = 0.31 in our summary is agreement between win-chance levels inside the task, not a link to pleasure or depression. Revontulet has 3 choices per game and no probability tiers, so it is a far smaller, gamified cousin of the task. |
| `rounds[].choice_ms`, `trace_start_ms` | Fault lines shown to the tap that picked one; the half-circle appearing to the first touch on it. | No study number. Plain decision and start times, kept so the choice and the trace can be read alongside how long the person took to begin. |
| `rounds[].trace_ms`, `trace_length_pt`, `trace_mean_speed_pt_s`, `trace_peak_speed_pt_s`, `trace_lifts` | How the half-circle was traced: time, path length, mean and peak finger speed, lifts. The half-circle turns a quarter turn each round. | Drawing kinematics: a tablet study (N = 40: 20 youths with a DSM-5 depressive disorder, mean age 14.65, and 20 healthy controls) used tasks described in our summary as including tracing four single-stroke semicircles rotated clockwise in 90° steps (a reviewer who read the paper's abstract found handwriting of a repeated cursive letter with a pen, and could not confirm the semicircles; we could not open the paper), and reached 82.5% subject-level classification accuracy from 3 velocity-related features. The summary adds that velocity scaling (peak velocity scaled to stroke length) gives stronger and more consistent effects than raw velocity. Different device, age group and task length from Lanvi. |
| `rounds[].pull_pt`, `pull_hold_ms` | How far the spark was pulled back, and how long it was held before the release. | No study number. Kept as a second, simple motor measure next to the trace. |
| `rounds[].embers_shown`, `embers_hit`, `bonus` | Embers that flashed on a hard shot, how many were tapped in time, and whether the surprise extra reward came. | Zero-punishment design and positive reward prediction errors: missing never costs progress; successes give larger-than-expected rewards. A design rule from the summary, not a measure with a study number. The ember reaction times already go into `reaction_ms`. |
| `strokes[].ms`, `length_pt`, `mean_speed_pt_s` | The slow strokes over the sleeping fox. | In-game swipe speed: the summary reports average swipe speed correlating inversely with PHQ-8 (ρ = −0.405, p < 0.001) in a mobile game study (Chitale et al. 2025, JMIR Mental Health: 78 people analysed, in its puzzle game; an exploratory study of roughly 450 measures with uncorrected p values). The strokes are also the C-tactile haptic moment of the game; that part is about the feel, not a measure. |
| `touch_radius_mean_pt`, `touch_radius_var` | Mean and variance of the touch radius (`UITouch.majorRadius`). | Swipe pressure variance correlating inversely with PHQ-8 (ρ = −0.326, p = 0.004) in the same kind of study. **An iPhone does not report pressure here**: touch radius is only a stand-in, and the number may not behave like pressure. |
| `broke_through_s` | Seconds from the start to the wall bursting (null if the fox's own spark had to finish it). | No study number. Marks when the celebration (and the app's pleasant moment for the face) happened. |
| `fox_finished` | True when time ran out and the fox's own spark broke the wall, so `broke_through_s` was not the person's doing. | No study number. Tells apart games where the person broke the wall from games where the fox finished it. |
| `pt_per_cm` | Screen points per centimetre on that phone. | Lets speeds in points per second be turned into cm per second. Speeds are still only comparable for one person on one phone: the half circle is laid out in points, so its real size differs by phone. |

## Own normal (clinician page only)

Four numbers are taken from each game (`revontulet_features` in `engine/clinic_engine/mind/activities.py`): the share of the three rounds where the harder crack was picked (none for a game cut short), the middle time to choose, and the tracing and stroke speeds in cm per second (none when the phone did not send `pt_per_cm`). Each is compared with two references, by the same robust rule as the other games (middle value and spread, with a floor):

- **The games just before**: at least 8 earlier Revontulet games, at most the last 20. This reference moves with the person.
- **The start**: games 3 to 12 (the first two are practice). This one does not move, so a slow slide cannot hide in it.

Either reference also needs its games to fall on at least 5 different days (`revontulet_min_days`). The first real games were ten in one evening: that is one sitting, not a usual range, and the page says so in place of any comparison.

Both count only games on the same phone (the same `pt_per_cm`, within 0.5): on a new phone they start again. The page labels a game only on the two speeds, at 2.5 or more either way, and a stroke speed needs at least two strokes. The harder crack has four possible values a game, so it is only compared over several games. A game sent without the measures never enters these references, and nothing here reaches the watchers or the judging of a day.

How the rules behave on generated games: `python -m clinic_engine.revontulet_check`. The generator's speeds and game-to-game variation are set from the first real games the team has (six Revontulet games from one person on one evening, 14 November: tracing about 14 cm/s varying by about 13% from game to game, strokes about 21 cm/s varying by about 29%). "Spread" scales that variation; 1.0 is as in those games. Measured on 5,000 runs of 24 games per row:

| Spread | Unchanged games labelled: recent | start | either | Unchanged people with three in a row: recent | start | Last three together: looks labelled | people labelled at least once in ten looks | Example's slide labelled slower in its last three games: one game, recent | one game, start | last three together |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 9% | 9% | 12% | 2% | 2% | 3% | 17% | 16% | 99% | 100% |
| 1.0 | 11% | 11% | 15% | 4% | 5% | 3% | 18% | 8% | 68% | 92% |
| 1.5 | 10% | 11% | 15% | 4% | 6% | 3% | 18% | 5% | 38% | 61% |
| 2.0 | 10% | 11% | 14% | 4% | 6% | 3% | 18% | 4% | 23% | 37% |

Three rules: one game against the games just before (cut 2.5), one game against the start (cut 2.5), and the last three games together against the start. The third is a counting rule with no tuned number (`_three_together` in the check, the same lines in `app.js`): "slower" when each of the last three games is slower than all start games but at most one, "faster" the same way. If nothing changes and games are independent, that happens in 8 of 286 looks, 2.8%, which the table reproduces. All three rules are measured on the same last three games of the slide. What the table says:

- The false-label rates barely depend on the spread (they are a little lower at half the real variation, where the floors still hold), because every rule measures a game against the person's own games. About one unchanged game in nine gets a label per reference; with 10 start games this cannot be made much smaller without losing the slide.
- One game against the recent games sees little of a slow slide, because the recent games slide along. That is what the start is for.
- Three games together catch the slide more often than one game, at a lower false rate per look. Over ten looks about one unchanged person in six still sees that line at least once, and since looks share games and the same start, it tends to stay once it appears. A single label is weak evidence.
- At twice the variation of the first real games even three together catch the example's large slide in fewer than 4 runs in 10.

How the rules got here: with the first, steadier generator (3% variation) the numbers looked much better (0 to 13% false labels, 81 to 100% of the slide caught against the start). The first real games showed four times the speed and four to ten times the variation, and the table above replaced the earlier one. A first version of the third rule compared the mean of three games with the start in units of the start's spread; a reviewer showed its cut was weaker than it looked (the start's own middle value is only estimated from ten games), it labelled 7% of unchanged looks, and it was replaced by the counting rule. The numbers 8 and 2.5 were picked on generated games. The real games behind the generator were played in ten minutes by a team member: how much a patient varies from day to day over weeks may be more or less. The generated games lack what real play has: odd games (a distracted game, another finger), learning, and slow drifts from day to day, which all add labels. The example's slide is large (speeds fall by over a third); a smaller change would be caught less often, and that has not been measured. These are properties of the generator, not of people.

The example person for the page: `python -m clinic_engine.seed_revontulet` (Example V-001, 24 generated games with the slide written in). Its run of the generator (`EXAMPLE_SEED`) was picked to show the usual case: in about 9 of 10 runs the headline reads "slower than the start", as it does there; in the rest it reads "not clearly different", and the first run tried was one of those. About 7 in 10 such runs also show at least one yellow tracing point in the chart (counted by `revontulet_check`); this one shows none, so it is cleaner than most in that way too (two of its games do carry a stroke label in the table). The page says this in the example's first lines.
## How a Revontulet game is read (since the first real games, 14 November 2026)

Nine real games from one team member on one evening (an iPhone 15 Pro, a dim hotel lobby) are the only real play there is. They changed five things in `engine/clinic_engine/mind/activities.py`. None of it is validated; each rule is a reading of those nine games.

- **A game with its measures is never compared with one sent without them.** Both are `focus` records. A real game set after ten records that had only taps came out 2.9 away from normal on error rate only because the counting differs. Now the own normal of every focus number and of the face uses earlier games of the same game only (`game_kind`), and the model that reads a session is shown only those.
- **Reaction time needs two ember taps**: a game has six embers at most, and the real ones had four or six. The spread still needs four. In seven of the nine real games no ember was tapped at all, so most real games have no reaction time; whether people do not see the embers or cannot catch them is a question for the game, not something the server can repair.
- **Error rate is embers missed over embers shown**, none when no ember flashed (three easier cracks). The launches are left out: they cannot go wrong. Its floor is wide: one ember of four stays inside the usual range.
- **A game where the wall burst is played to the end.** Seven of the nine real games ended `user_stopped` with all three rounds played and the wall broken: the person left during the strokes. That is counted as finished for the share of finished sessions, the session reading and Lanvi's line; `ended_by` is stored as sent.
- **Coached rounds and flicks are left out of the four numbers.** A traced round without `trace_start_ms` is one the first-time coach showed; it is left out of the time to choose and the tracing speed (its choice still counts). A touch on the fox shorter than 0.3 s is not a stroke (the real games had ones of 121 and 158 ms).

What the nine games say about the table above: from game to game the tracing speed varied by about 13 to 15% and the stroke speed by about 30%. The generator now varies its games by that much, so the row at spread 1.0 is the one that matches them, and the rows at 1.5 and 2.0 show what happens if real play varies more. One person, one evening, one phone: a first look, not a norm.

Every generated example person now plays Revontulet (`seed.revontulet_game`), with speeds, times and screen scale on the scale of those nine games. One thing is kept kinder than real play on purpose: the example people tap most embers, so their games have reaction times and the two-week example can show "slower reactions".
