# Revontulet: what each measure is, and the study idea behind it

Revontulet is Lanvi's 90-second game: pick a fault line in the ice (easy or hard), trace a half-circle, pull the spark back like a slingshot and let go, tap the embers on a hard shot, then stroke the sleeping fox. The app sends it as a `focus` record with one extra object, `revontulet` (shape and limits: `docs/api/README.md` and `openapi.json`, `RevontuletIn`).

The server stores these numbers and shows them to the clinician. **It does not use them** to judge a day, pick suggestions or count XP. Since 14 November it does compare four of them with the person's own earlier Revontulet games, for the clinician page only: see "Own normal" at the end. Nothing here is validated in Lanvi; the studies below are where each idea comes from, with the numbers as the research summary for this game gives them. On 14 November the two effort papers (Treadway 2009 and 2012) and the swipe paper (Chitale 2025) were read on PMC and the rows below corrected; of the tablet handwriting paper (Džepina et al. 2025, Applied Sciences) only the abstract was read, through Crossref: 20 youths with depression and 20 controls wrote the cursive letter "l" repeatedly; 177 features were cut to 3; 82.5% of subjects were sorted correctly. The abstract does not mention semicircles.

| Measure | What it is | Study idea behind it |
|---|---|---|
| `rounds[].effort` | Easy (thin low crack, one shot) or hard (thick vein, two embers, a bigger reward). | The Effort-Expenditure for Rewards Task (EEfRT, Treadway et al. 2009, PLoS ONE): on each of about fifty trials a person picks a low-effort "easy task" for a small fixed reward or a high-effort "hard task" for a higher, variable reward. Among 61 students, those reporting less pleasure chose the hard task a little less often (correlations around −0.3), on some questionnaires and win chances and not on others; one pleasure questionnaire (SHAPS) showed nothing, and hard choices also fell with trial number. Treadway et al. 2012: 20 patients with depression chose the hard task less often than 15 controls; 17 of the patients were on antidepressants, and among the patients a higher depression score went with more hard choices, a longer episode with fewer. The r = 0.31 in our summary is agreement between win-chance levels inside the task, not a link to pleasure or depression. Revontulet has 3 choices per game and no probability tiers, so it is a far smaller, gamified cousin of the task. |
| `rounds[].choice_ms`, `trace_start_ms` | Fault lines shown to the tap that picked one; the half-circle appearing to the first touch on it. | No study number. Plain decision and start times, kept so the choice and the trace can be read alongside how long the person took to begin. |
| `rounds[].trace_ms`, `trace_length_pt`, `trace_mean_speed_pt_s`, `trace_peak_speed_pt_s`, `trace_lifts` | How the half-circle was traced: time, path length, mean and peak finger speed, lifts. The half-circle turns a quarter turn each round. | Drawing kinematics: a tablet study (N = 40: 20 youths with a DSM-5 depressive disorder, mean age 14.65, and 20 healthy controls) used tasks described in our summary as including tracing four single-stroke semicircles rotated clockwise in 90° steps (a reviewer who read the paper's abstract found handwriting of a repeated cursive letter with a pen, and could not confirm the semicircles; we could not open the paper), and reached 82.5% subject-level classification accuracy from 3 velocity-related features. The summary adds that velocity scaling (peak velocity scaled to stroke length) gives stronger and more consistent effects than raw velocity. Different device, age group and task length from Lanvi. |
| `rounds[].pull_pt`, `pull_hold_ms` | How far the spark was pulled back, and how long it was held before the release. | No study number. Kept as a second, simple motor measure next to the trace. |
| `rounds[].embers_shown`, `embers_hit`, `bonus` | Embers that flashed on a hard shot, how many were tapped in time, and whether the surprise extra reward came. | Zero-punishment design and positive reward prediction errors: missing never costs progress; successes give larger-than-expected rewards. A design rule from the summary, not a measure with a study number. The ember reaction times already go into `reaction_ms`. |
| `strokes[].ms`, `length_pt`, `mean_speed_pt_s` | The slow strokes over the sleeping fox. | In-game swipe speed: the summary reports average swipe speed correlating inversely with PHQ-8 (ρ = −0.405, p < 0.001) in a mobile game study (Chitale et al. 2025, JMIR Mental Health: 78 people analysed, in its puzzle game; an exploratory study of roughly 450 measures with uncorrected p values). The strokes are also the C-tactile haptic moment of the game; that part is about the feel, not a measure. |
| `touch_radius_mean_pt`, `touch_radius_var` | Mean and variance of the touch radius (`UITouch.majorRadius`). | Swipe pressure variance correlating inversely with PHQ-8 (ρ = −0.326, p = 0.004) in the same kind of study. **An iPhone does not report pressure here**: touch radius is only a stand-in, and the number may not behave like pressure. |
| `broke_through_s` | Seconds from the start to the wall bursting (null if the fox's own spark had to finish it). | No study number. Marks when the celebration (and the app's pleasant moment for the face) happened. |
| `fox_finished` | True when time ran out and the fox's own spark broke the wall, so `broke_through_s` was not the person's doing. | No study number. Tells apart games where the person broke the wall from games where the fox finished it. |
| `pt_per_cm` | Screen points per centimetre on that phone. | Lets speeds in points per second be turned into cm per second. Speeds are still only comparable for one person on one phone: the half circle is laid out in points, so its real size differs by phone. |

## Own normal (clinician page only)

Four numbers are taken from each game (`revontulet_features` in `engine/clinic_engine/mind/activities.py`): the share of the three rounds where the harder crack was picked (none for a game cut short), the middle time to choose, and the tracing and stroke speeds in cm per second (none when the phone did not send `pt_per_cm`). Each is compared with two references, by the same robust rule as the other games (middle value and spread, with a floor):

- **The games just before**: at least 8 earlier Revontulet games, at most the last 20. This reference moves with the person.
- **The start**: games 3 to 12 (the first two are practice). This one does not move, so a slow slide cannot hide in it.

Both count only games on the same phone (the same `pt_per_cm`, within 0.5): on a new phone they start again. The page labels a game only on the two speeds, at 2.5 or more either way, and a stroke speed needs at least two strokes. The harder crack has four possible values a game, so it is only compared over several games. Sparks and snow games never enter these references, and nothing here reaches the watchers or the judging of a day.

How the rule behaves on generated games: `python -m clinic_engine.revontulet_check`. "Spread" is how much the two speeds vary from game to game, as a multiple of the example's 10% either way; nobody has measured it on people. Measured on 5,000 runs of 24 games per row, with the rule as it is (8 games, 2.5):

| Spread | Unchanged games labelled, recent | start | either | Unchanged people with three in a row, recent | start | Example's slide, last two weeks, labelled slower: recent | start |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.1% | 0.1% | 0.2% | 0.0% | 0.0% | 70% | 100% |
| 2 | 7.4% | 7.1% | 9.9% | 1.0% | 1.1% | 32% | 98% |
| 3 | 9.4% | 9.8% | 13.2% | 2.5% | 3.9% | 17% | 81% |

How the rule got here, on the same generator: with 5 games, a cut of 1.5 and a label on time to choose as well, 37% of unchanged games were labelled at spread 1. With 8 games, 2.0 and two speeds it was 2% at spread 1 but 15 to 17% at spreads 2 and 3. The cut of 2.5 was chosen from this table. It trades away the moving reference's power to see a slow slide, which is what the start is for. The generated games also lack what real play has: odd games (a distracted game, another finger) and slow drifts from day to day. Both add labels, and drifts add runs of them, so the three-in-a-row figures are the most optimistic in the table. The example's slide is large (tracing falls by more than a third); a smaller change would be caught less often, and that has not been measured. These are properties of the generator, not of people.

The example person for the page: `python -m clinic_engine.seed_revontulet` (Example V-001, 24 generated games with the slide written in).
